A FEW THINGS WORTH KNOWING

Good gauge. Better beginnings.

Simple formulas. A little understanding.
More time for the good part.

01 / MAKE A SWATCH

A small square saves a lot of unraveling.

Use the same yarn, hook or needles, and stitch pattern you’ll use for the project. Make a swatch larger than the area you plan to measure.

02 / WASH & MEASURE

Measure the fabric you’ll actually wear.

Wash and dry your swatch the way you’ll care for the finished piece. Count stitches and rows in the center, away from the edges.

03 / FIND YOUR COUNT

A repeat is worth keeping.

Stitches = desired width × swatch stitches ÷ swatch width. Rows use the same formula with height. Adjust stitch counts to your pattern repeat.

Gauge reference: Craft Yarn Council · How to measure. Examples and calculations on this page are our own.

How do I calculate stitches from gauge?+

Divide the number of swatch stitches by the measured swatch width, then multiply by the desired project width. At 18 stitches per 4 inches, a 20-inch width needs 90 stitches. At 24 rows per 4 inches, 24 inches needs 144 rows.

What does “multiple of 6 + 2” mean?+

Your stitch count must be 6 × a whole number + 2. For a raw count of 90, the nearest valid count is 92. Enter 6 as the stitch repeat and 2 as extra stitches. Those extra stitches are part of the pattern’s fabric count, not automatically turning chains.

Can I use this for crochet and knitting?+

Yes, the stitch-density formula works for either craft. Use a swatch in the actual stitch pattern and keep the same units for your swatch and project. Knitting cast-on and crochet foundation instructions still come from your pattern.

Will this resize an entire garment pattern?+

It estimates the fabric stitch and row counts for a rectangular area. It does not grade garment shaping, armholes, necklines, stitch charts or fitted ease. Use the result as a planning aid and check the pattern construction.

Why doesn’t the predicted size exactly match my target?+

You can only work whole stitches and rows, and a pattern repeat limits which counts are valid. We show the predicted size at your measured gauge after rounding. Tension and blocking can still change the finished dimensions.
